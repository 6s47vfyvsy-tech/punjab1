KHALSA RAJ • 1801–1849

Punjab
from its golden age to today

The shared story of a historic homeland—from Maharaja Ranjit Singh’s Lahore Darbar through annexation, resistance, Partition, and the modern Punjabs of India and Pakistan.

Begin with the Khalsa Raj ↓

Panj-ab • the land of five rivers

One civilization, two modern states

Punjab’s modern history begins before the border. Lahore and Amritsar, Multan and Ludhiana, Rawalpindi and Patiala formed parts of one connected region. Punjabi-speaking Sikhs, Muslims, Hindus, Christians and others shaped its courts, farms, armies, shrines, poetry, trade and cities.

The opening chapter

The golden age of the Khalsa Raj

After taking Lahore in 1799 and assuming the title of Maharaja in 1801, Ranjit Singh united many Sikh misls and built a powerful state centered on Lahore. At its height, the kingdom extended from the Sutlej toward the Khyber region and included Kashmir, Multan and Peshawar.

The Lahore Darbar was Sikh-led but religiously diverse. Sikh, Muslim and Hindu officials served in government, while European officers helped modernize parts of the army. Revenue, diplomacy, fortifications, artillery and a disciplined Khalsa army sustained the kingdom.

Chronology

From sovereignty to divided Punjab

1947

The border cut through a homeland

Partition divided British Punjab into West Punjab in Pakistan and East Punjab in India. It triggered one of the largest mass migrations in modern history and catastrophic communal violence. Muslims moved west; Sikhs and Hindus moved east; families lost homes, land, neighbors and cultural worlds.

Partition was not only a political event. It reshaped language, memory, city life, religious landscapes and the demographic character of both Punjabs.

After 1947

Two Punjabs, connected histories

INDIA

Punjab, India

Refugee resettlement, the building of Chandigarh, linguistic reorganization in 1966 and the Green Revolution transformed the state. The late 1970s through early 1990s brought political conflict, militancy, counterinsurgency, Operation Blue Star and the 1984 anti-Sikh massacres. Later decades saw renewed migration, cultural globalization and major debates over farming, water and employment.

  • Capital shared at Chandigarh
  • Punjabi mainly written in Gurmukhi
  • Major centers include Amritsar, Ludhiana, Jalandhar and Patiala
PAKISTAN

Punjab, Pakistan

West Punjab absorbed millions of Muslim refugees and became Pakistan’s demographic, agricultural and industrial heartland. Lahore remained a major center of politics, education, publishing, film and architecture. Canal agriculture, rapidly growing cities and large manufacturing districts shaped the province, while heritage preservation and Punjabi-language advocacy continue to matter.

  • Capital at Lahore
  • Punjabi commonly written in Shahmukhi
  • Major centers include Lahore, Faisalabad, Rawalpindi, Multan and Gujranwala
“Punjab” survives not only as territory, but as language, music, memory, faith, food and family across borders.

Punjab today • 2026

A border exists. A culture still travels.

Contemporary Punjab is shaped by farming and industry, crowded cities, environmental stress, migration, digital media and a global diaspora. Indian and Pakistani Punjabis live under different political systems, yet music, poetry, religious pilgrimage, family memory and shared language repeatedly cross the boundary.

Editorial approach

This is a concise public-history website. It treats Punjab as a shared historical region and avoids presenting either national story as the whole story. Violent and contested events are summarized carefully; the bibliography offers starting points for deeper study.
